# Editorial lenses: criteria, severities and traps

Read once per session before the first review. The SKILL.md steps say *what* to do; this
file says *how to judge*. Every note still needs a verbatim `articleQuote` and a fix.

## point

- Write the piece's point in one sentence before anything else. If you can't, that is a
  **must** (anchor it to the paragraph where the nut graf should go, usually ¶2–¶4).
- The nut graf (why this matters, now) should land by paragraph 3 or 4. Later is a
  **should**; absent is a **must**.
- The headline and dek (¶0) must promise what the body delivers. A headline that says
  more than the piece shows is a **must** (and, if it is a factual overreach, also "Flag
  for fact-check"). A headline that undersells is a **could**.
- Opinion pieces: is the argument stated, or only implied? Reports: is the news in the
  first two paragraphs?

## structure

- **Buried lede:** the most newsworthy fact or strongest line sits below ¶4 → **must** if
  it changes what the piece is about, else **should**. The fix names where to move it.
- **Repeated beats:** two paragraphs making the same point → **should**; suggest which to
  cut or merge.
- **Wandering:** a section that doesn't serve the point → **should** (cut or tie back).
- **Order:** chronology where logic would serve, or a reveal held back for no reason.
- **Ending:** trails off, repeats the intro, or introduces a new claim → **could**
  (should if it changes the takeaway).
- Long pieces: do subheads match what follows them?

## fairness

- The subject's **strongest** response (not the weakest) should be present and given
  weight near the criticism, not buried at the end. Missing → **must** when the piece
  criticizes a named person or organization, else **should**. You are not checking
  whether a response exists in the record (that's the fact-check); you are noting that
  the piece doesn't carry one.
- **Asserted motive:** "to hide", "in a bid to", "deliberately" without shown support →
  **should** (must if it is the piece's main charge).
- **Groups:** descriptions the brand wouldn't stand behind, sweeping generalizations,
  loaded labels → **must** if demeaning, else **should**.
- Read the brand's roles and voice: a brand that writes as advocates is still fair to the
  people it names.

## proportion

- Hot verbs and adjectives that outrun the support: "slams", "admits", "secret",
  "bombshell", "destroys", "unprecedented" → **should** (could if the support is plainly
  there in the text). Suggest the plain verb ("said", "criticized", "undisclosed").
- Certainty that outruns attribution ("will" where the piece only shows a proposal).
- **Factual doubts:** "Flag for fact-check: …" + why you doubt it → **should**. Never say
  the fact is wrong, and never offer a replacement fact.

## clarity

- Jargon or acronyms without a definition on first use → **could** (should if central).
- Numbers without context (no comparison, base rate, time frame or unit) → **should**.
- Sentences over ~35 words that hide the actor; passive voice that hides who did it
  ("mistakes were made") → **should** when it hides responsibility, else **could**.
- Pronouns with unclear antecedents, a "this" with nothing to point to.

## voice

- Compare to the pack's `voice` (tone, person, formality, banned words, signature moves)
  and `exemplars`. Note drift only where the voice profile or exemplars show it; never
  your own taste. Usually **could**; **should** if a whole section reads like another
  outlet or breaks an explicit voice rule (a banned word, the wrong person).
- Roles: does the piece write from the vantage its Role describes? (A "reporter who reads
  contracts before press releases" shouldn't lean on a press release.)

## reader

- Mark the paragraph where a smart, busy reader would stop, and why (a wall of
  background, a detour, an unexplained term) → **should**.
- The unanswered question: what does the reader want to know that the piece never says
  (what happens next, what it costs, who decides, what they can do)? → **should**
  (**must** if it is the obvious first question).

## Writing the note

- **What's wrong** (one sentence), **why it matters to the reader** (one sentence), the
  **fix** (concrete; example wording marked "Suggestion: …").
- Quote the smallest verbatim span that locates the problem. Copy it from the pack's
  paragraph text exactly, including punctuation and curly quotes.
- One habit, one note: name the worst instance and say "also in ¶5, ¶9".
- Praise goes in the summary, one line, specific ("The contract timeline in ¶6–¶8 is the
  piece's spine; keep it").
